Why this election matters

This was a rematch. Chris Hipkins led Labour to defeat in 2023, when National took about 38% of the party vote and Labour about 27%. Three years later the two parties are level on roughly 29% each, so the result turns on the smaller parties rather than on either giant.

A caretaker government, for now

Until the results are settled and a new government is formed, New Zealand is under a caretaker government. Ministers keep running the country day to day, but by convention they avoid major new decisions. Luxon remains prime minister in the meantime.

A thin margin

New Zealand is governed by whoever can command a majority in Parliament, usually through coalitions. On the preliminary count, National, ACT and NZ First hold 67 of 131 seats, one over the line. A handful of special votes could move that.

Seven parties, and a bigger Parliament

The Commission projects 131 MPs rather than the usual 120. That happens when a party wins more electorate seats than its party vote entitles it to, which is called an overhang. Te Pāti Māori is the clearest case: just 0.57% of the party vote, but five Māori electorates. Opportunity crossing the 5% threshold adds a seventh party, and a new voice in any coalition talks.

What was at stake

Cost of living dominated the campaign. Other live questions included tax, housing and the role of the Treaty of Waitangi in law and policy, which separates ACT, Te Pāti Māori and others sharply. Te Pāti Māori also went in with internal splits, and a former MP ran for a different party in Te Tai Tokerau.

How the voting system works

  1. Two votes. Each voter casts an electorate vote for a local MP and a party vote for a party.
  2. The party vote sets the shares. Seats in Parliament are shared out in proportion to party votes, so 29% of the party vote means roughly 29% of the seats.
  3. The 5% rule. A party needs 5% of the party vote or one electorate seat to take list seats. That is how Te Pāti Māori is in Parliament on 0.57%.
  4. Overhang. A party that wins more electorate seats than its share allows keeps them all, so Parliament grows past 120. That is why it is projected at 131.
  5. Māori electorates. There are seven, and voters on the Māori roll choose these instead of general electorates.
  6. Forming a government. A government needs the confidence of a majority of Parliament, and in a 131-seat house that is 66. No single party is near it, so coalition talks decide who governs.

Beyond New Zealand

The result also matters outside the country: it shapes where a small, trade-dependent democracy leans on security, trade and climate.

A foreign policy reset on the ballot

Since 2023 the coalition has lifted defence spending, tied New Zealand’s military more closely to Australia’s, and explored AUKUS Pillar II, a technology partnership with Australia, the UK and the US. None of this went to voters in 2023. Supporters say New Zealand had underspent on defence. Critics say it weakens New Zealand’s independent foreign policy. Labour backed the extra spending but opposes AUKUS, as do the Greens and Te Pāti Māori.

What is Five Eyes, and why the Greens matter

Five Eyes is a decades-old arrangement among the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand to share intelligence, mostly intercepted communications. New Zealand’s part is run by its GCSB agency. It is not a military alliance. The Greens’ 2026 manifesto calls for withdrawing from it and for staying out of AUKUS. Labour says New Zealand should stay. The Greens are the biggest of Labour’s likely partners (a projected 16 seats), and the two parties have differed on this for years. A left-led government would have to settle it in coalition talks.

The China balance

China is New Zealand’s largest trading partner, and relations have cooled. The next government decides how to balance security alignment against that trade.

Trade

A free trade agreement with India took effect on 20 October and cuts tariffs on 95% of New Zealand’s exports to India. The next government inherits it, and the push to find markets beyond China.

Climate and Paris

The coalition cut New Zealand’s 2050 farm methane target almost in half, from a 24–47% reduction to 14–24%. Its 2035 Paris pledge is the smallest step up from 2030 that the agreement allows, according to critics, and Climate Action Tracker says it is not on a 1.5°C path. The parties now split on Paris itself: National says New Zealand should stay in, while ACT and NZ First want to leave or renegotiate. Labour has dropped farm emissions pricing from its campaign, which the Greens still want.

If it had gone the other way

On the preliminary count the governing bloc (National, ACT and NZ First) holds 67 seats against 64 for Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity. Had a few seats moved, a Labour-led government would have been the likely outcome.

  • At home. Labour dropped farm emissions pricing from its campaign, the Greens still want it, and the Treaty of Waitangi would have been handled very differently from the coalition’s approach. A left bloc would have needed to bargain over all of it.
  • Abroad. Labour opposes AUKUS and the Greens want out of Five Eyes. A left-led government would have had to settle those splits in coalition talks, with Australia, the US and the UK watching.
  • Party votes, electorate wins and seats are related but not identical.
  • Governments are assembled through parliamentary confidence and coalition agreements, not by the biggest party alone.
  • The special vote count can change party seats and the overhang, so treat every number here as provisional.
